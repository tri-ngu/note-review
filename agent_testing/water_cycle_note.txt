The water cycle, also called the hydrologic cycle, describes the continuous movement of water on, above, and below the surface of the Earth. Water is neither created nor destroyed in this process — it simply changes form and location, moving between the atmosphere, land, and oceans in a repeating cycle driven by solar energy and gravity.

Evaporation is the process by which liquid water changes into water vapor and rises into the atmosphere. The sun's heat provides the energy needed for evaporation, primarily from oceans, lakes, and rivers, which together account for the vast majority of water entering the atmosphere. Warmer temperatures increase the rate of evaporation, which is why evaporation is fastest in tropical regions and during summer months. Approximately 90 percent of the moisture in the atmosphere comes from evaporation off the surface of oceans, with the remaining 10 percent contributed by other sources such as lakes, rivers, and transpiration.

Transpiration is a related but distinct process in which plants release water vapor from their leaves into the atmosphere. Water absorbed by plant roots travels up through the stem and is released as vapor through small pores called stomata. Because evaporation and transpiration are difficult to measure separately in vegetated areas, scientists often combine them into a single term: evapotranspiration. A single large tree can transpire hundreds of liters of water on a hot, sunny day.
Once precipitation reaches the ground, it follows one of several paths. Surface runoff occurs when water flows over the land's surface into streams, rivers, and eventually back into oceans or lakes, completing the visible portion of the cycle. Runoff is the primary way that liquid water returns to bodies of water, and it can carry sediment, nutrients, and pollutants along with it as it moves downhill.

As water vapor rises higher into the atmosphere, it encounters progressively cooler air. Condensation is the process by which water vapor cools and changes back into liquid water droplets. These droplets gather around tiny particles of dust, salt, or smoke in the air, forming clouds. Condensation is essentially the reverse of evaporation, and it is the process responsible for the formation of clouds and fog.


Precipitation is the primary way water returns from the atmosphere to the Earth's surface. On average, the Earth receives about 505,000 cubic kilometers of precipitation each year, roughly 78 percent of which falls over the oceans and the remaining 22 percent over land.

When the water droplets within a cloud become too heavy for rising air currents to support them, they fall back to Earth as precipitation. Precipitation can take several forms depending on atmospheric temperature: rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Rain is the most common form of precipitation in warmer climates, while snow and sleet occur when temperatures near the ground are at or below freezing. Hail forms when strong updrafts within storm clouds carry water droplets through freezing layers of air multiple times before they fall.
Infiltration is the process by which water soaks into the ground, moving through soil and rock layers to become groundwater. Groundwater is stored in underground formations called aquifers, which can hold water for long periods of time — in some cases, thousands of years. Groundwater slowly moves through the ground and can eventually resurface through springs or be drawn up by plant roots, contributing to transpiration described earlier. Groundwater also feeds into rivers and lakes during dry periods when surface runoff is minimal, helping to sustain ecosystems and water supplies between rainfalls.

Collection refers broadly to the accumulation of water in oceans, lakes, rivers, and groundwater reservoirs, where it will eventually evaporate again and restart the cycle. Because the water cycle has no true beginning or end, collection is often considered the closing stage of one cycle and the starting point of the next, as accumulated water is once again exposed to solar energy and begins to evaporate.
